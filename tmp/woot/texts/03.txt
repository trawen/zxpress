YThe last couple of years of Speccy homebrew releases have felt like some kind of personal wish-fulfilment dream for the 15-year-old me. All the games I most loved playing in seaside arcades on summer holidays, but which never got decent home conversions for
my beloved rubber-keyed buddy, have shown up one after the other in the form of some truly superb ports: Mister Kung Fu, Maze Death Rally-X, Terrapins, Horace And The Robots and most recently the tremendous out-of-the-blue appearance of Astro Blaster.
Not only are they all wonderfully playable games in the here and now, they also make the past somehow retrospectively better. Because suddenly there's an alternate universe of the mind where we all got to enjoy them at the time instead of the relatively tatty efforts that actually came out.REVIEW: Mr Do! (Page 2)
But Universal's colourful and action-packed manic-mining monster-masher from 1982, Mr Do!, was such a big ask for the Spectrum that despite it being a huge hit widely ported to every other format under the sun, nobody had even bothered to take a semi-serious Speccy stab at it until 2006, when Bob Smith produced the gorgeous-looking Farmer Jack In Harvest Havoc. But while it was deftly done, it didn't do Do!To experience the iconic bouncing ball, classic Can-Can music and the clever, extra-life-hunting ruleset we had to wait another 13 years for Adrian Singh and Mark R Jones to somehow cram the perky polka-dotted Pierrot into our beloved black rubber buddy.And what an absolutely phenomenal feat of cramming it is - capturing not only the look and sound of the coin-op but the feel, to the point where you almost forget that you're not playing the real thing, so similar and familiar are the rhythms and patterns.
But then, to all intents and purposes, you are.

Review by Rev. Stuart Campbell.

Strangely enough, there are few text adventures for the Spectrum set in WW2, a potentially endless source of inspiration.
It is worth noting, then, that Andy McDermott's Resistance not only takes its subject matter from the most tragic period of world history, but also gives the player the opportunity of impersonating a really unusual alter ego.It is 1943 and the protagonist is Mari Roux, a French 17-year old girl living in a small village, whose father was killed during the Nazi invasion. Her mother died of grief soon after; her older brother was forced to work in Germany, and has not been heard of for a year. No wonder, then, that she developed a deep hatred for the invaders.Offering herself to help the local Resistance, Mari receives one day a note from a man called Jean Agreste, the leader of the local undercover freedom fighters. Agreste will assign her several missions of sabotage and counter-propaganda, such as covering Nazi posters with Resistance ones, exposing a Nazi informer, and even stealing the Nazi flag from the town hall and throwing it

Iinto a public toilet! After each mission, Mari has to meet Agreste again, who then assigns her another mission, until something very bad happens...The game is then divided into several sub-adventures, and the player, as Mari, will have to perform the assigned tasks without being discovered by the ubiquitous Nazi thugs and their local bootlickers, against which she will even have to fight for her life.The game is authored with The Quill and text-only. It is not very difficult, so it can be suitable for beginners. Puzzles are linear and do not require an excessive amount of ingenuity. Veterans, on the other hand, will appreciate the unusual and intriguing setting.

QBy Denis Grachev, Retrosouls
QGLUF is essentially an action packed painter-platform game with baddies that patrol horizontally, vertically, jump about, chase you and use lifts. That may be an already crowded genre, but GLUF has enough quality, playability, and personality to make it noteworthy. It's also a multicolour game, and of the
18 released so far, it's the most fluid and action packed of the lot. It is down to clever design, but there is nothing here that feels compromised by the burden of a multicolour engine. It even manages vertical scrolling nicely.GLUF, the "Tesla Frog", must charge up on the battery, and release it onto the dead cells. This concept started as a UDG BASIC game devised during 1997, and what it finally became would have been inconceivable to anyone at the time. Hell - it's impressive enough now and we've had 8 years to habituate to multicolour trickery.

Over the 25 levels, we are treated to two great AY tracks that are catchy enough to dislodge even the most stubbornly embedded "Come on Eileen" from your head. The most notable is a version of "Leva's Polka", a Finnish folksong that somehow became the Leekspin internet meme.If the game is lacking anything, then it's variety in the level design. It's fine, and it does develop as the game progresses, but there is a slight saminess to some of it. It certainly didn't stop me wanting to complete it.Denis, like all Russian coders, is probably doping in order to achieve these feats (just look at the 2019 Yandex comp!), but that doesn't change the fact that it's an astonishing achievement. There have been several evolutionary jumps in multicolour games over recent years, and GLUF is the latest. So here and now, we should plant a golden spike in the ground to mark it.

IIn a small village near Bremen, in 1846, Gunter Bikemayker stood forlorn in his workshop. Clutching his redundancy notice and divorce papers, he looked at his tools and felt an intense, burning hatred for mankind. It was at this point that Gunter resolved to make the worst bicycle in the world, to punish humanity for wronging him so badly. After a few weeks' work, Gunter stood back and examined his creation. He called it the "HasseBike". It had square wheels, a spike for a seat, chocolate handlebars, no gears (or drivetrain), and as it moved a mechanical hand poked the rider repeatedly in the kidneys. Gunter succeeded - it was undoubtedly the worst bike the world had ever seen, and it held this record until the 12th July 2006, when the Sinclair A-Bike was unleashed upon the world.Now, none of that is true, but it hopefully makes a mildly amusing introduction to my experience of the A-bike. I bought mine in 2018, while online and at the thin end of a bottle of wine.

IAt best I hoped for a fun curio I could ride about, and maybe use for short commutes during busy rail journeys. When the bike arrived, I was impressed by its style and compactness. Folding and unfolding the A-Bike is not intuitive, but once you learn the trick it is quick and easy. So far so good - then I tried riding it. The tiny 6" wheels are made of rock-solid rubber, so everything except a perfectly planar surface is unbearable. And that's the good bit over with, because this bike is PROPERLY unsafe. It's a good job you can't go very fast, because the tiniest pothole will have you upended on the floor. The frame is so small, that anyone of average height and above will look ridiculous on it, and struggle to peddle with bent legs.However, I was determined to stick with it, because I paid good money for it, and the gearing and brakes do deserve some credit for being well-made. So I took it on a train journey.

II walked a short way to the station with the bike already folded. This is where the A-Bike impresses - it's light to carry and easy to stow on even the busiest train. It was when I alighted to ride it the problems started. I felt frankly ridiculous on this slow, rattly bike. I don't know how much it really stood out, but I was so embarrassed that I was glad it was getting dark. Then the lightest drizzle started, and I found the A-Bike's latest flaw. The slick wheels and tiny frame do not want to balance or go forward when the ground is slightly wet - it wants to go sideways and slam you on your hip. After the second time this happened, I lay on the ground with the drizzle licking my face, and wondered how engineering's answer to SQIJ could ever be deemed fit for sale, and why did I love Sinclair products so much? I had no answer, other than to stand up, fold-up the A-Bike and walk to my destination with it - after all - this bike carries great...
